People overcomplicate this. **Buying gold means choosing a form (physical metal, a fund, or metal inside a retirement account), buying it from a reputable source at a fair markup, and storing it somewhere safe.** Everything else is detail. This guide walks through each decision in the order you'll actually face it.

## The three ways to own gold

There is no single "right" way — there's the way that fits what you're trying to do. Most people are deciding between three.

<Figure caption="The three common forms of gold ownership, compared. Premiums are typical ranges over the spot gold price and vary by dealer and product.">

| Form | What it is | Typical premium | Best when you want |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical bullion | Coins and bars you hold | 3%–8% over spot | Metal in your own hands |
| Gold ETF or fund | Shares that track the gold price | ~0.2%–0.6% per year | Easy buying and selling |
| Gold in an IRA | Approved metal held by a custodian | Setup + storage fees | Tax-advantaged retirement holding |

</Figure>

**Physical bullion** is what most people picture: a one-ounce coin like an American Gold Eagle or Canadian Maple Leaf, or a small bar. You own the actual metal. The tradeoff is the premium — the markup over the raw "spot" gold price — and the responsibility of storing it.

**A gold ETF** (exchange-traded fund) is a share you buy in a brokerage account. It tracks the gold price without you ever touching metal. It's the simplest to buy and sell, but you don't hold anything physical, and you pay a small annual fee.

**Gold inside an IRA** lets you hold approved physical metal in a tax-advantaged retirement account. It's the most paperwork, and it's a path people near retirement ask about most — we cover it in the [gold IRA rollover guide](/guides/gold-ira-rollover-guide).

<Callout label="Quick rule of thumb">
If you want gold *in your hand*, buy bullion. If you want gold *in your portfolio* with the least friction, buy an ETF. If you want gold *in your retirement account*, that's an IRA — a separate process.
</Callout>

## Understanding spot price and premium

Two numbers decide what you pay. The first is the **spot price** — the live market price for an ounce of pure gold. The second is the **premium** — the dealer's markup over spot, which covers minting, shipping, and profit.

A common coin might carry a 4%–6% premium; a generic bar often runs lower, around 2%–4%; and small fractional coins (a tenth of an ounce) can run 10% or more because the minting cost is spread over less metal. The premium is the single biggest variable you control. **Compare premiums, not just headline prices.**

## How to buy physical gold safely

1. **Pick the product.** For a first purchase, a recognized one-ounce government coin (Eagle, Maple Leaf, Krugerrand) is the easiest to buy and later sell.
2. **Choose a reputable dealer.** Use an established national bullion dealer or a long-standing local coin shop. Check that pricing updates with the live spot price and that the premium is stated clearly.
3. **Confirm the total.** Read the all-in price: metal + premium + shipping + any card surcharge. Bank transfer is usually cheapest.
4. **Take delivery and verify.** When it arrives, check the weight and that packaging is sealed. Keep your receipt — it matters when you sell.

### Red flags to walk away from

- High-pressure phone sales pushing "rare" or "collectible" coins at huge markups.
- A premium far above the ranges above with no clear reason.
- A dealer who won't show you the spot price they're working from.

The doomsday-ad version of this niche makes money on fear and confusion. A fair dealer makes money on a transparent, modest premium. **If someone is selling urgency, that's the product — not the gold.**

## Storing what you buy

Physical gold needs a home you trust. The realistic options:

- **A quality home safe**, bolted down and fireproof — fine for smaller amounts.
- **A bank safe deposit box** — secure, low cost, but not insured by the bank, so add a rider to your policy.
- **Allocated third-party vault storage** — the metal is stored, insured, and held in your name. Best for larger amounts; you pay an annual fee.

For IRA gold, storage isn't optional — the metal must sit with an approved custodian, which is part of why that route has its own rules.

## How much gold is reasonable

There's no universal number, and anyone who gives you one without knowing your situation is guessing. As a frame of reference, a common diversification range people discuss is **5%–10% of a portfolio** in gold — enough to matter if other assets fall, not so much that you're betting the farm on one metal. Where you land is a personal call, ideally one you make with a professional who knows your full picture.
