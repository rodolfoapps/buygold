People overcomplicate this. **Buying gold means choosing a form (physical metal, a fund, or metal inside a retirement account), buying it from a reputable source at a fair markup, storing it somewhere safe, and keeping records for tax time.** Everything else is detail. This guide walks through each decision in the order you'll actually face it, with the real numbers attached — no fear, no countdown timers.

It's long because buying gold has more moving parts than the ads admit, and getting one of them wrong costs real money. Read it once and you'll know more than most people who already own gold.

## The three ways to own gold

There is no single "right" way to own gold — there's the way that fits what you're trying to do. **Most people are choosing between three: physical bullion, a gold fund, or gold inside a retirement account.** Each solves a different problem.

<Figure caption="The three common forms of gold ownership. Premiums are typical ranges over the spot gold price and vary by dealer, product, and quantity.">

| Form | What it is | Typical cost over spot | Best when you want |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical bullion | Coins and bars you hold | 3%–8% premium to buy | Metal in your own hands |
| Gold ETF or fund | Shares that track the gold price | ~0.2%–0.6% per year | Easy buying and selling |
| Gold in an IRA | Approved metal held by a custodian | Setup + storage + premium | A tax-advantaged retirement holding |

</Figure>

**Physical bullion** is what most people picture: a one-ounce coin like an American Gold Eagle or a Canadian Maple Leaf, or a small bar. You own the actual metal outright. The tradeoffs are the premium — the markup over the raw "spot" price — and the responsibility of storing and insuring it yourself.

**A gold ETF** (exchange-traded fund) is a share you buy in an ordinary brokerage account. It tracks the gold price closely without you ever touching metal. It is the simplest way to buy and sell, often within seconds, but you don't hold anything physical, and you pay a small annual expense ratio for the convenience.

**Gold inside an IRA** lets you hold approved physical metal in a tax-advantaged retirement account. It carries the most paperwork and the most ongoing fees, and it's the route people near retirement ask about most. It has its own rules, so it gets its own [gold IRA rollover guide](/guides/gold-ira-rollover-guide).

<Callout label="Quick rule of thumb">
If you want gold *in your hand*, buy bullion. If you want gold *in your portfolio* with the least friction, buy an ETF. If you want gold *in your retirement account*, that's an IRA — a separate process with its own paperwork.
</Callout>

The rest of this guide focuses on **physical bullion**, because that's where the decisions — and the chances to overpay — actually live. If a fund is your route, the price section below still matters; the storage and authentication sections won't.

## Coins or bars?

For most first-time buyers, coins are the easier choice, but bars cost a little less per ounce. **Government-minted coins are easier to buy, sell, and verify; bars carry lower premiums but can be slightly harder to resell.** Here's how they actually compare.

<Figure caption="Coins vs. bars for a typical retail buyer.">

| | Government coins | Private-mint bars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Premium over spot | Higher (3%–6%) | Lower (1%–4%) |
| Recognizability | Very high — anyone will buy them | Good for known refiners only |
| Resale ease | Easiest | Slightly harder |
| Counterfeit risk | Lower (sophisticated security features) | Higher for off-brand bars |
| Best for | First-timers, smaller amounts | Larger amounts, lowest cost per ounce |

</Figure>

If you're just starting, a recognized **one-ounce government coin** is the path of least regret. The most common, all roughly one troy ounce of gold:

- **American Gold Eagle** — the U.S. Mint's flagship; 22-karat (alloyed with copper for durability) but contains a full ounce of gold. Backed by the [U.S. Mint](https://www.usmint.gov).
- **American Gold Buffalo** — the Mint's 24-karat (.9999 fine) coin for buyers who want pure gold.
- **Canadian Gold Maple Leaf** — .9999 fine, known worldwide, strong anti-counterfeit features.
- **South African Krugerrand** — the original modern bullion coin; 22-karat, widely traded.
- **Austrian Philharmonic** — Europe's most popular bullion coin, .9999 fine.

### A word on fractional coins

Fractional coins — a half, quarter, or tenth of an ounce — give flexibility but cost more per ounce of gold. **A tenth-ounce coin can carry a premium of 10% or more, because the cost of minting it is spread over far less metal.** They're handy if you want smaller, more divisible pieces, but for pure value, full-ounce coins and bars give you the most gold for your dollar.

### Bullion vs. numismatic (collectible) coins

This is the distinction that protects your wallet. **Bullion coins are bought for their metal content; numismatic coins are bought for rarity and condition, and they carry huge, subjective markups.** High-pressure sellers love steering beginners into "rare" or "graded" coins at two or three times their melt value, then framing it as a smart move. Unless you are a serious collector who knows the market, **buy bullion, not numismatics.** The metal is the point.

## Spot price and premium: what you actually pay

Two numbers decide your cost. **The spot price is the live market price for an ounce of pure gold; the premium is the dealer's markup over spot.** Understanding both is the single best defense against overpaying.

The **spot price** moves constantly during market hours and is set in the global wholesale market. The widely watched benchmark is the LBMA Gold Price, published by the [London Bullion Market Association](https://www.lbma.org.uk). When a headline says "gold hit a record," it's quoting spot.

The **premium** is everything added on top: minting or refining cost, the dealer's margin, shipping, and payment processing. It is where dealers compete, and where you save or lose money.

| | Generic 1 oz bar | 1 oz government coin | 1/10 oz coin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spot price (example) | \$2,400 | \$2,400 | \$240 |
| Premium | 3% (\$72) | 5% (\$120) | 12% (\$29) |

| Cost per oz of gold | \$2,472 | \$2,520 | \$2,690 |

</Figure>

The lesson from the table: **compare the all-in price per ounce, not the headline price.** A coin that "looks" similar can cost meaningfully more once the premium and the coin size are accounted for. Note too that the spot price in the table is an illustration — check the live number before you buy.

<Callout label="Key takeaway">
The premium is the part you control. A fair premium on common bullion is roughly **2%–8%** depending on the product and quantity. Anything far above that needs a clear reason, or you walk.
</Callout>

## Where to buy gold safely

Buy from established dealers with transparent, live pricing — and skip anyone selling urgency. **The two reliable channels are reputable online bullion dealers and long-standing local coin shops.** Each has its place.

**Online dealers** usually offer the lowest premiums and the widest selection, with pricing that updates against the live spot price in real time. The tradeoffs are shipping time and the need to trust delivery. Look for a long track record, published buy-and-sell prices, secure insured shipping, and real customer service.

**Local coin shops** let you walk out with metal in hand and build a relationship with someone who will also buy it back later. Premiums can run a touch higher to cover the storefront, but the immediacy and the in-person trust are worth it to many buyers.

### How to vet a dealer in two minutes

- They show the **spot price** they're working from, and the premium is stated plainly.
- Their **buy price and sell price** are both published, so you can see the spread.
- They have a verifiable history — years in business, real reviews, an industry association membership.
- Payment is straightforward, with **bank transfer** offered as the cheapest option.
- No one pressures you, and no one calls you back three times.

### Red flags to walk away from

- High-pressure phone sales pushing "rare," "limited," or "collectible" coins at large markups.
- A premium far above the normal range with no clear explanation.
- A dealer who won't show you the spot price they're quoting against.
- Free-gift or "price-locked" gimmicks that distract from the actual cost per ounce.
- Anyone telling you the dollar collapses next month and you must act today.

The doomsday-ad corner of this niche makes money on fear and confusion; a fair dealer makes money on a transparent, modest premium. **If someone is selling urgency, that's the product — not the gold.**

## Paying for it

How you pay changes the price. **Bank wire or ACH transfer is almost always the cheapest; credit cards add a surcharge of roughly 3%–4%.** Many dealers post two prices — a lower "cash/wire" price and a higher card price — because card processing eats their margin. For a large purchase, the wire discount can easily outweigh the hassle of setting up the transfer.

One legal note worth knowing: cash purchases above **\$10,000** trigger federal reporting requirements for the dealer (IRS Form 8300). This is routine and nothing to fear — it simply means very large cash deals aren't private. Normal wire and card purchases of any size are unremarkable.

## Making sure it's real

Stick to recognized products from reputable dealers and authentication rarely becomes a problem. **Government coins carry sophisticated security features, and reputable dealers guarantee authenticity.** Still, it's worth knowing the basics:

- **Buy known products** — common government coins and bars from major refiners are the hardest to fake and the easiest to verify.
- **Check the specs** — gold has a precise weight and dimensions for each coin; a fake is usually off on one of them. A simple jeweler's scale and calipers catch most.
- **Use a magnet** — gold is not magnetic; if a "gold" piece is drawn to a magnet, it isn't pure gold.
- **For larger purchases**, a handheld ultrasonic or conductivity tester gives certainty, and many dealers offer assay-card bars sealed with a certificate.

## Storing what you buy

Physical gold needs a home you trust, and the right choice depends on how much you hold. **The realistic options are a home safe, a bank safe deposit box, or insured third-party vault storage.**

<Figure caption="Storage options compared.">

| Option | Cost | Security | Insurance | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Home safe | One-time purchase | You manage it | Add a rider to home policy | Smaller amounts, quick access |
| Bank safe deposit box | ~\$40–\$200/yr | High | Not bank-insured — add your own | Mid-size holdings |
| Allocated vault storage | ~0.5%–1%/yr | Highest | Fully insured, in your name | Larger holdings |

</Figure>

- **A quality home safe**, bolted down and rated for fire, suits smaller amounts and gives you instant access. Don't advertise that you own gold, and keep records elsewhere.
- **A bank safe deposit box** is secure and cheap, but the bank does not insure the contents, so add a rider to your homeowner's or renter's policy.
- **Allocated, insured vault storage** holds the metal in your name, fully insured, for an annual fee. It's the standard for larger holdings — and for IRA gold, where storage with an approved depository is *required*, not optional. (More on that in the [gold IRA rollover guide](/guides/gold-ira-rollover-guide).)

Whatever you choose, **insure it** and tell one trusted person where it is and how to access it. Gold that no one can find helps no one.

## Taxes when you buy and sell

The buy is simple; the sell is where taxes show up. **In the U.S., physical gold held over a year is taxed as a "collectible," with a maximum long-term capital gains rate of 28% — higher than the rate on most stocks.** Held a year or less, gains are taxed as ordinary income. There's no tax simply for owning gold; the bill comes only when you sell at a profit.

A few practical points:

- **Keep your receipts.** Your cost basis (what you paid, including the premium) is subtracted from the sale price to figure the gain. No receipt, no proof of basis.
- **Dealers report some sales.** Selling certain quantities of specific bullion can trigger a 1099-B from the dealer; gains are your responsibility to report either way.
- **Losses can offset gains.** If you sell at a loss, it can offset other capital gains.

This is general information, not tax advice — rules and rates change, and your situation is yours. Confirm current figures with a tax professional before you sell. (The full disclaimer is at the bottom of this page.)

## How much gold is reasonable

There's no universal number, and anyone who gives you one without knowing your finances is guessing. **A commonly discussed diversification range is 5%–10% of a portfolio in gold** — enough to matter if other assets fall, not so much that you're betting your future on a single metal. Where you land depends on your age, your other holdings, and how you sleep at night. It's a personal call, ideally made with a professional who can see your whole picture.

## Buying all at once, or a bit at a time

You don't have to deploy everything in one purchase. **Two sensible approaches exist: buy your full position at once if you've decided on a price you're comfortable with, or "average in" with regular smaller purchases to smooth out the price you pay.**

Averaging in — buying a fixed dollar amount on a schedule, say monthly or quarterly — means you buy more ounces when the price is low and fewer when it's high, and you never have to guess the perfect moment. The tradeoff is slightly higher total premiums, since smaller orders sometimes carry a higher markup than one large order. For most first-time buyers worried about buying at a peak, averaging in is the calmer path. For a buyer who's been waiting for a target price, a single purchase is fine. There's no wrong answer; there's only the one that lets you sleep.

## How to sell gold later

You'll want to sell someday, and knowing how beforehand prevents a bad deal under pressure. **Sell back to a reputable dealer at a fair spread, bring your records, and never sell in a hurry to the first cash-for-gold storefront you see.**

A few practicals:

- **Sell common bullion to bullion dealers**, online or local — the same kind you bought from. They pay closest to spot for recognized coins and bars.
- **Understand the spread.** Dealers buy below spot and sell above it; that gap is their margin. On common bullion the buyback is usually within a few percent of spot. Get two quotes if you can.
- **Avoid "we buy gold" jewelry counters and pawn shops** for bullion — they typically pay well under melt value.
- **Bring your purchase records** so you can prove your cost basis for taxes, and remember the collectibles rate (up to 28% long-term) applies to any gain.
- **Don't sell in a panic.** The worst prices are taken by people who feel they must sell *today*.

The easier your gold is to sell, the better — which is the quiet argument for sticking to recognized coins and bars in the first place.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Overpaying on premium** by not comparing the all-in price per ounce.
- **Buying numismatic coins** thinking they're a better "investment" than bullion.
- **Loading up on fractional coins**, paying a steep premium for divisibility you may not need.
- **Skipping insurance** on home-stored metal.
- **Losing the paperwork**, which inflates the eventual tax bill.
- **Acting on fear** — buying in a panic at a spike, instead of on a plan.

## Should a first-timer buy gold or silver?

Many beginners weigh gold against silver, and the honest answer is that they serve slightly different roles. **Gold is steadier and more compact in value; silver is cheaper per ounce, more volatile, and bulkier to store.** Neither is "better" — they fit different temperaments.

A few practical differences. Silver's low price per ounce makes it easy to start small, and it tends to swing harder than gold in both directions, which appeals to some and unnerves others. But silver is far less dense in value: storing \$25,000 of silver takes dramatically more space — and more storage cost — than the same value in gold. Silver also usually carries a higher percentage premium and, in many states, different sales-tax treatment. For a first purchase focused on steady diversification, gold is the simpler choice; for a buyer who wants more upside potential and doesn't mind the swings and the bulk, a slice of silver alongside gold is reasonable. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is the cheapest way to buy gold?**
The cheapest physical gold is generally a bar or generic round from a reputable online dealer, paid by bank wire, where premiums run lowest (often 1%–4%). For exposure without holding metal, a low-cost gold ETF is cheaper still on the way in, though it charges a small annual fee.

**Is it better to buy gold coins or gold bars?**
Coins are better for first-timers and smaller amounts because they're easy to sell and verify; bars are better for larger amounts because they cost less per ounce. Many buyers hold some of each.

**How much gold can I buy without reporting?**
You can buy any amount; there's no purchase limit. Reporting rules apply to the *dealer*, not a tax on you — cash payments over \$10,000 trigger a federal form, and certain bullion sales generate a 1099-B when you sell.

**Where should I store my gold?**
Smaller amounts in an insured home safe; larger amounts in a bank safe deposit box or an insured third-party vault. Always insure it, and never store IRA gold at home — that breaks the rules.

**Do I pay tax when I buy gold?**
Some states charge sales tax on bullion and others exempt it, so it varies by location. You don't owe income tax for buying or holding gold — only when you sell at a profit, where the collectibles rate (up to 28% long-term) applies.
