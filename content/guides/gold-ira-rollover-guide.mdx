This is the question people near retirement ask most, and it's where the worst sales pressure lives. So let's keep it plain. **A gold IRA rollover moves money from an existing retirement account — a 401(k) or traditional IRA — into a self-directed IRA that holds approved physical gold, without you taking the cash and triggering taxes.** Done as a direct transfer, it's a routine, tax-free move. Done carelessly, it can cost you income tax plus penalties.

This guide is thorough on purpose, because the costly mistakes here are all avoidable once you know the rules. We'll cover what a gold IRA actually is, the one transfer method to use, which accounts and metals qualify, the storage rule that trips people up, every fee involved, the tax treatment, and how to spot the sales tactics aimed at retirees.

## What a gold IRA actually is

A gold IRA is a self-directed retirement account that holds physical metal instead of only stocks and funds. **It has the same contribution limits and tax treatment as an ordinary IRA — the difference is what it's allowed to hold and the parties required to make that legal.**

Three players make a gold IRA work, and understanding them clears up most of the confusion:

- **The custodian** administers the account, handles the paperwork, and reports to the IRS. It must be an IRS-approved trustee that handles precious metals — not every IRA custodian does.
- **The dealer** sells you the approved metal. You choose the products; the custodian pays the dealer from your IRA funds.
- **The depository** stores the physical metal in an approved, insured vault. **You cannot keep IRA gold at home** — the metal must be held by the depository on the account's behalf.

That triangle — custodian, dealer, depository — is the whole structure. The fees attached to it are why a gold IRA costs more to run than a plain index-fund IRA, which we'll itemize below.

## Why people consider it

The appeal is specific: holding a hard asset inside tax-advantaged retirement money. **People near retirement often want a slice of their savings in something that doesn't move in lockstep with stocks and bonds, held in the same tax-sheltered wrapper as the rest of their retirement funds.** The story usually rhymes with the reader's own — watching a portfolio get hammered in a downturn and wanting ballast that behaves differently next time.

That's a reasonable instinct. It's also exactly the instinct that high-pressure marketers exploit, which is why the rest of this guide is as much about avoiding traps as it is about the mechanics.

## Direct vs. indirect rollover — get this right

There are two ways to move the money, and this single choice matters more than anything else on the page. **A direct transfer moves funds custodian-to-custodian and is essentially risk-free; an indirect rollover sends the money to you first and opens the door to taxes and penalties.**

<Figure caption="Direct vs. indirect rollovers. When in doubt, choose direct.">

| | Direct transfer | Indirect (60-day) rollover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How money moves | Custodian to custodian; you never touch it | Check comes to you; you redeposit it |
| Tax / penalty risk | Very low | High if you miss the deadline |
| Deadline | None | 60 days to redeposit |
| Withholding | None | Plan may withhold 20% up front |
| Frequency limit | Unlimited | One indirect IRA-to-IRA rollover per 12 months |

</Figure>

A **direct transfer** (a trustee-to-trustee transfer) sends the funds straight from your old account to the new custodian. You never receive the money, so there is nothing to tax and no clock to beat. **This is the route to use in almost every case.**

An **indirect rollover** sends *you* a check, and you then have **60 days** to deposit the full amount into the new account. Miss that window and the IRS treats the whole sum as a distribution — ordinary income tax, plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty if you're under 59½. According to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions), the rules also limit you to one indirect IRA-to-IRA rollover in any 12-month period.

There's a second trap with indirect rollovers from a 401(k): the plan is generally required to **withhold 20%** for taxes before sending you the check. To roll over the full balance and avoid tax on that 20%, you'd have to make up the withheld amount out of your own pocket, then wait to recover it at tax time. It's a needless headache.

<Callout label="The one mistake to avoid">
Don't take the check yourself unless you have a specific reason. A **direct, custodian-to-custodian transfer** sidesteps the 60-day rule, the 20% withholding, and the once-per-year limit in a single move.
</Callout>

## Which accounts can you roll over?

Most common retirement accounts can be moved into a gold IRA, with one timing caveat for current employers. **Eligible sources typically include a 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), the federal Thrift Savings Plan, and traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs.**

The caveat is the workplace plan you're still contributing to. **A 401(k) with a *former* employer can usually be rolled over anytime; a 401(k) with your *current* employer often can't be moved until you reach age 59½ or leave the job** — that's a plan rule, not an IRS one, so check your plan's terms. Roth accounts can roll into a Roth gold IRA to preserve their tax-free treatment; mixing pre-tax and Roth money is where you want a professional's eye.

## Which metals are allowed

The IRS sets minimum purity standards, and not every coin qualifies. **IRA gold must be at least 99.5% pure, from an approved mint or refiner, and collectible coins are generally barred.** The standards differ slightly by metal:

<Figure caption="Minimum fineness for IRA-eligible precious metals (IRS rules).">

| Metal | Minimum purity | Common eligible examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gold | 99.5% (.995) | American Gold Eagle*, Gold Buffalo, Canadian Maple Leaf |
| Silver | 99.9% (.999) | American Silver Eagle, Canadian Silver Maple Leaf |
| Platinum | 99.95% (.9995) | American Platinum Eagle |
| Palladium | 99.95% (.9995) | Canadian Palladium Maple Leaf |

</Figure>

\*The **American Gold Eagle** is a deliberate exception: it's 22-karat (about 91.67% pure) yet specifically permitted by statute, because it contains a full ounce of gold and is U.S.-government issued.

What's **not** allowed matters just as much. **Rare, graded, or "collectible" numismatic coins are generally prohibited in an IRA** — which is notable, because over-marked collectible coins are exactly what aggressive sellers push at retirees. If a salesperson steers you toward "rare" coins "perfect for your IRA," that's a reason to slow down. Your custodian can confirm any specific product's eligibility before you buy.

## The storage rule that trips people up

IRA metal must be stored by an approved depository — never at your house. **The so-called "home storage gold IRA" is a marketing idea, not a safe one; storing IRA metal at home is treated by the IRS as a distribution, with the taxes and penalties that follow.** The metal has to sit in an approved, insured depository, held on the account's behalf, to keep its tax-advantaged status.

You usually get a choice between **segregated** storage (your specific coins and bars kept separate) and **commingled** storage (your metal pooled with others' of the same type, with your share recorded). Segregated costs a bit more. Either way, it's a real, ongoing fee — which brings us to the part the ads gloss over.

## The fees nobody mentions up front

A gold IRA costs more to run than a plain IRA, and seeing every line item beforehand is the mark of a fair provider. **Expect a setup fee, an annual custodian fee, an annual storage fee, and the dealer premium on the metal itself.**

<Figure caption="Typical gold IRA fees. Exact amounts vary by provider — always get the full schedule in writing.">

| Fee | Typical range | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account setup | ~\$50–\$100 | One-time |
| Annual custodian fee | ~\$75–\$300 | Administration and reporting |
| Annual storage fee | Flat fee or ~0.5%–1% of holdings | Charged by the depository |
| Dealer premium | A few percent over spot | The markup on the metal — see [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold) |

</Figure>

None of these are disqualifying, but they add up, especially on a smaller account where flat fees eat a bigger share. Ask for the **complete fee schedule in writing** before you commit. A straight, itemized answer is a good sign; a runaround or a "we'll cover the fees if you buy these special coins" pitch is not.

## The rollover, step by step

1. **Open a self-directed IRA** with a custodian that handles precious metals. Compare fee schedules first.
2. **Request a direct transfer** from your 401(k) or existing IRA into the new account. The new custodian usually helps initiate this so the money never touches your hands.
3. **Choose approved metal** through a reputable dealer, and confirm each product's IRA eligibility with the custodian before buying.
4. **Settle and store.** The custodian pays the dealer from your IRA funds, and the metal ships to the approved depository in the account's name.
5. **Keep your paperwork.** Confirmations and statements matter at tax time and when you eventually take distributions or sell.

## Taxes, RMDs, and taking the money out later

A gold IRA follows the same tax rules as any IRA of its type, with one wrinkle when you take metal out. **A traditional gold IRA grows tax-deferred and is taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal; a Roth gold IRA is funded with after-tax money and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.**

Two points specific to metals:

- **Required minimum distributions (RMDs)** apply to traditional gold IRAs once you reach the RMD age, just as with any traditional IRA. Because you can't withdraw a fraction of a coin neatly, satisfying an RMD sometimes means selling metal inside the account or taking an "in-kind" distribution of coins — worth planning for in advance.
- **Distributions can be in-kind or in cash.** You can have the actual metal shipped to you (an in-kind distribution), or have the custodian sell it and distribute cash. Either way, a traditional account's distribution is taxable income in that year.

The collectibles tax rate discussed in [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold) applies to metal you hold *personally*, not to metal inside the IRA — inside the account, normal IRA tax rules govern. As always, confirm current rules with a tax professional; the bottom-of-page disclaimer applies.

## How long it takes

A gold IRA rollover isn't instant, but it's not slow either. **From opening the account to metal sitting in the depository, a direct transfer typically takes about one to three weeks**, depending mostly on how quickly your old plan releases the funds.

A rough timeline:

- **Day 1–2:** Open the self-directed IRA with your chosen custodian (paperwork, often online).
- **Day 2–10:** The direct transfer is requested and the old custodian releases funds. This is usually the longest step, and it's out of your hands — some 401(k) plans move in days, others take a couple of weeks.
- **Day 10–14:** With funds settled, you select approved metal with the dealer and the custodian pays for it.
- **Day 14–21:** The metal ships to the approved depository and posts to your account.

If anyone promises it'll all happen in 24 hours, be skeptical — and if a provider is rushing you to "lock in" coins before the transfer even clears, that's a sales tactic, not a deadline.

## Gold IRA vs. buying physical gold yourself

A gold IRA is not the only way to own gold for retirement, and it isn't always the best one. **The IRA wins on tax treatment; buying physical gold directly wins on simplicity, cost, and control.** Which matters more depends on you.

<Figure caption="Gold IRA versus holding physical gold outside a retirement account.">

| | Gold IRA | Physical gold you hold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax treatment | Tax-advantaged (deferred or Roth) | Taxed as a collectible on sale |
| Ongoing fees | Custodian + storage every year | None beyond your own storage |
| Storage | Required approved depository | Your choice — home, box, or vault |
| Access | Restricted until retirement age | Immediate |
| Complexity | Higher — rules and paperwork | Low |
| Best for | Tax-sheltered retirement money | Flexible, simple, hands-on ownership |

</Figure>

For money that's already inside a retirement account and meant to stay there until retirement, the IRA's tax shelter is the draw. For money you might want sooner, or if you simply value holding the metal yourself with no annual fees, buying physical gold directly — covered in [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold) — is often the cleaner choice. Plenty of people do both.

## The honest pros and cons

A gold IRA suits some people and is overkill for others, and a balanced look beats a sales pitch. **It offers tax-advantaged diversification into a hard asset, at the cost of higher fees, more complexity, and an asset that pays no income.**

**In its favor:** real diversification inside a tax shelter, a hard asset that doesn't move in lockstep with stocks, and the same tax benefits as any IRA.

**Against it:** setup, custodian, and storage fees that a plain index-fund IRA doesn't have; no dividends or interest while you hold it; the price swings covered in [what moves the price of gold](/guides/what-moves-the-price-of-gold); and a thicket of rules where mistakes are penalized. For many people a small allocation makes sense; for some, simple physical gold or a low-cost fund outside an IRA is the better fit.

## Traditional or Roth gold IRA?

The same pre-tax-versus-after-tax choice you'd weigh for any IRA applies to gold, and it comes down to when you'd rather pay the tax. **A traditional gold IRA gives you the tax break now and taxes withdrawals later; a Roth gold IRA is funded with after-tax dollars and gives you tax-free qualified withdrawals.**

The rough logic most people use: if you expect to be in a *lower* tax bracket in retirement than you are today, the traditional route — deduct now, pay later at a lower rate — tends to come out ahead. If you expect a *higher* bracket later, or you simply want the certainty of tax-free withdrawals and no required minimum distributions, the Roth route is appealing. A rollover from a traditional 401(k) typically lands in a traditional gold IRA to stay tax-free; converting that to a Roth is possible but creates a taxable event in the year you convert. This is squarely a question for a tax professional who knows your bracket and your plans.

## What happens to a gold IRA when you die

A gold IRA passes to the beneficiaries you name, just like any IRA. **Naming a beneficiary on the account keeps the metal out of probate and lets it transfer directly**, which is one more reason to keep your paperwork current. Inherited IRA rules — including how quickly heirs must draw the account down — are detailed and have changed in recent years, so an heir should get professional guidance rather than guessing. The practical step for you now is simple: make sure the beneficiary designation on the account is filled in and up to date.

## Red flags and high-pressure tactics

Retirees are the prime target for gold IRA marketing, so know the warning signs. **Be wary of free-gift offers, "limited-time" pressure, pushes toward expensive collectible coins, and anyone promising the metal will only go up.**

- **"We'll cover your fees"** in exchange for buying specific high-premium coins — the markup more than pays them back.
- **Collectible or "exclusive" coins** pitched as IRA-perfect, when bullion at a fair premium is what you actually want.
- **Fear-based urgency** — the dollar collapses, act now — designed to short-circuit comparison shopping.
- **Vague fees** or a refusal to put the schedule in writing.

### How to vet a custodian and dealer

- Confirm the custodian is an **IRS-approved trustee** with a real track record.
- Get the **full fee schedule in writing** and compare two or three providers.
- Stick to **standard bullion** that clearly meets the purity table above.
- Verify the **depository** is a recognized, insured facility, and choose between segregated and commingled storage knowingly.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I roll over my 401(k) into gold without penalty?**
Yes — a direct, trustee-to-trustee transfer into a self-directed gold IRA moves the money without taxes or penalties. The penalties only appear if you take a distribution yourself and miss the 60-day redeposit deadline.

**Can I store my gold IRA at home?**
No. IRA metal must be held by an approved depository. Storing it at home is treated as a distribution and can trigger income tax and a 10% penalty if you're under 59½.

**What gold is IRA-approved?**
Gold that is at least 99.5% pure from an approved mint or refiner — for example American Gold Buffalos and Canadian Maple Leafs, plus the American Gold Eagle by specific exception. Collectible and graded coins generally are not allowed.

**How much does a gold IRA cost?**
Beyond the metal's premium, expect a one-time setup fee (~\$50–\$100), an annual custodian fee (~\$75–\$300), and an annual storage fee (a flat amount or roughly 0.5%–1% of holdings). Always get the schedule in writing.

**What's the difference between a transfer and a rollover?**
A transfer moves money directly between custodians and is the safest route. A rollover, in the strict sense, often means the money passes through your hands with a 60-day deadline. When people say "rollover" loosely they may mean either — just make sure yours is done custodian-to-custodian.

**How long does a gold IRA rollover take?**
A direct transfer usually takes about one to three weeks from start to finish, with the old plan releasing the funds being the slowest step. Be wary of anyone promising it happens in a day or pressuring you to buy coins before the transfer clears.

**Can I add more money to a gold IRA each year?**
Yes — beyond the rollover, you can make annual contributions up to the standard IRA limit (the same limit that applies to any IRA, with a catch-up amount once you're 50 or older). Those contributions follow the same purity and storage rules as the rest of the account.

**Is a gold IRA a good idea?**
For some people it is, and for others it isn't. It offers tax-advantaged diversification into a hard asset, but it carries higher fees and more rules than a plain IRA, and gold pays no income. A small allocation suits many; for some, simple physical gold outside an IRA fits better. Decide with a professional who sees your full picture.
