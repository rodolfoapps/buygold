The number in the headline isn't the number on your invoice. **The spot price is the live wholesale price for an ounce of pure gold; the premium is the markup a dealer adds on top, and together they make your real, all-in cost.** Confuse the two and you can overpay by a wide margin without noticing. Get them straight and you'll buy as smartly as a dealer.

<GoldPriceStack />

## What the spot price is

The spot price is the global wholesale price for immediate delivery of one troy ounce of gold. **It's set in the worldwide market and quoted around the clock, and it's the number you see when a headline says "gold hit a record."** A troy ounce is about 31.1 grams, slightly heavier than the ounce on your kitchen scale.

Spot moves constantly during market hours, driven by interest rates, the dollar, and the other forces covered in [what moves the price of gold](/guides/what-moves-the-price-of-gold). For buying purposes, the key fact is simpler: **you, as a retail buyer, never pay exactly spot.** Spot is the wholesale floor; the premium is what sits on top of it.

## What the premium is

The premium is everything a dealer adds to the spot price to make a finished product worth selling. **It covers minting or refining, the dealer's margin, shipping, insurance, and payment processing.** It's quoted either as a dollar amount or, more usefully, as a percentage over spot.

The premium isn't a scam — it's how the business works, the same way a store sells a can of soup for more than the wholesale cost. The point isn't to avoid the premium; it's to pay a *fair* one and to know what fair looks like.

## What a fair premium looks like

Fair premiums vary by product, and the pattern is consistent enough to memorize. **Common bullion runs roughly 2%–8% over spot; bars are at the low end, government coins in the middle, and small fractional coins much higher.**

<Figure caption="Typical premium ranges by product. These vary with the dealer, the quantity, and how busy the market is.">

| Product | Typical premium over spot | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic 1 oz bar or round | 1%–4% | Cheapest to produce |
| 1 oz government coin | 3%–6% | Minting + brand + liquidity |
| Fractional coin (1/10 oz) | 10%+ | Minting cost spread over less metal |
| "Rare" / collectible coin | 50%–200%+ | Subjective; avoid as a beginner |

</Figure>

Two forces push premiums around in real time. **High demand raises premiums** — during a buying panic, premiums on coins can spike even when spot barely moves, because dealers run short of physical product. And **bigger orders lower premiums** — buying ten coins usually costs less per ounce than buying one.

<Callout label="Key takeaway">
The premium is the part of the price you control. Compare the **all-in cost per ounce** across dealers and products — not the spot price, which is the same everywhere — and a fair premium on common bullion sits in the low single digits to mid-single digits.
</Callout>

## Why "per ounce" is the only fair comparison

Comparing two listings by their sticker price is a trap, because the products differ. **Always reduce the price to dollars per ounce of actual gold, then compare.** A tenth-ounce coin and a one-ounce coin can't be compared head to head until you do.

Here's the same idea as the worked example in [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold), boiled down: if spot is \$2,400, a 1 oz coin at a 5% premium costs \$2,520 per ounce, while a 1/10 oz coin at a 12% premium costs \$2,688 per ounce of gold. Same metal, very different value. The fractional coin buys you divisibility — the ability to sell a little at a time — but you pay for it.

## The spread: what it costs to buy *and* sell

The premium is only half the round trip. **Dealers sell above spot and buy back below it, and that gap — the spread — is the real cost of owning physical gold.** A dealer might sell a coin at 5% over spot and buy it back at 1% under spot, a 6-point round-trip cost.

This matters for two reasons. First, it means gold has to rise by more than the spread before you break even on a sale. Second, it rewards **liquid, recognizable products** — common coins and bars have tight spreads because everyone will buy them, while obscure or collectible items have wide spreads that quietly eat your money. It's the strongest practical argument for sticking to mainstream bullion.

## Premiums on funds and "paper" gold

If you buy a gold ETF instead of metal, the premium concept changes shape. **A fund has almost no upfront premium, but it charges a small annual expense ratio — often around 0.2%–0.6% — and trades at a tiny premium or discount to its gold value.** You skip minting and storage costs entirely, which is why funds are the cheapest way to get gold exposure on the way in. The tradeoff is that you don't hold metal. Which route fits is covered in [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold).

## How to avoid overpaying

- **Check the live spot price** before you shop, from any major quote source, so you have a baseline.
- **Convert every listing to cost per ounce** of gold and compare across dealers.
- **Favor low-premium, liquid products** — common coins and bars — unless you have a specific reason for fractional or collectible pieces.
- **Pay by bank transfer**, which usually beats the card price by 3%–4%.
- **Buy in calmer markets** when you can; premiums balloon during panics.
- **Ignore "rare coin" pitches** dressed up as smart buys — that's premium you'll never get back.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is gold more expensive than the spot price?**
Because spot is the wholesale price for raw metal. As a retail buyer you pay spot plus a premium that covers minting, the dealer's margin, shipping, and insurance — typically a few percent on common bullion.

**What is a good premium on gold?**
On common bullion, roughly 1%–4% for bars and 3%–6% for government coins. Fractional coins run 10% or more, and collectible coins carry markups so large that beginners should avoid them.

**Do you pay over spot when you sell gold too?**
No — when you sell, dealers buy *below* spot. The gap between their sell price and buy price is the spread, and it's the true round-trip cost of owning physical gold.

**Is it better to buy gold when premiums are low?**
Yes, all else equal. Premiums rise with demand, so buying in calmer markets and in larger quantities lowers your cost per ounce. Just don't let waiting for a perfect premium keep you from a plan you've decided on.
