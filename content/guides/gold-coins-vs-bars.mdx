It's the first real fork once you've decided to buy physical gold. **Coins are easier to buy, sell, and verify and carry a slightly higher premium; bars cost less per ounce but can be marginally harder to resell.** Neither is "better" — they suit different goals, and many people own some of each. Here's how to choose.

## The short answer

For most first-time buyers, coins win on practicality. **Government-minted coins are the most liquid, recognizable, and counterfeit-resistant form of gold, which makes them the safer default; bars make sense once you're buying larger amounts and want the lowest cost per ounce.** If you only remember one thing: start with common coins, graduate to bars as your holdings grow.

### Premium and cost per ounce

Bars are cheaper to make, so they cost less over spot. **A generic one-ounce bar might carry a 1%–4% premium, while a one-ounce government coin runs 3%–6%.** Over a large purchase that gap adds up, which is the main reason serious accumulators lean toward bars. For the full mechanics of premiums, see [spot price vs. premium](/guides/spot-price-vs-premium).

### Liquidity and resale

Coins are easier to sell, full stop. **A common government coin is recognized worldwide, so any dealer will buy it quickly at a tight spread.** Bars from major refiners (the well-known names) sell easily too, but an obscure bar can draw a lower offer or an authentication step. If you think you might sell in pieces over time, coins give you that flexibility.

### Counterfeit resistance

Modern government coins are hard to fake. **They carry precise weights, fine detailing, and increasingly sophisticated security features**, and their specifications are public, so a fake is easy to catch on a scale or with calipers. Bars from reputable refiners often come in tamper-evident assay cards with serial numbers. The risk concentrates in cheap, off-brand bars from unknown sources — which is a reason to buy bars only from recognized mints and trusted dealers.

### Sizes and divisibility

Coins come in convenient fractions; bars favor round, larger units. **You can buy gold coins in one ounce, a half, a quarter, even a tenth — handy if you want to sell a little at a time — while bars are most cost-effective in one-ounce and larger sizes.** Fractional coins cost more per ounce (the premium is spread over less metal), so the divisibility is a convenience you pay for.

## Popular choices

If you go with **coins**, the mainstream, easy-to-resell options are:

- **American Gold Eagle** — 22-karat, a full ounce of gold, U.S.-government issued.
- **American Gold Buffalo** — 24-karat (.9999) for those who want pure gold.
- **Canadian Gold Maple Leaf** — .9999 fine, strong anti-counterfeit features.
- **Krugerrand** and **Austrian Philharmonic** — widely traded worldwide.

If you go with **bars**, stick to recognized refiners and common sizes (1 oz and 10 oz are the most liquid for individuals). Look for an assay card or serial number, and buy from a dealer who guarantees authenticity.

## A note on collectible coins

Don't confuse bullion coins with numismatic ones. **Bullion coins are priced for their metal; numismatic (collectible) coins are priced for rarity and condition, with large, subjective markups.** Beginners are often steered toward "rare" coins as a supposedly smart move — it usually isn't, unless you're a serious collector. For buying gold as gold, bullion coins and bars are the point.

## Which should you buy?

- **Choose coins if** you're new, want maximum resale ease, value the option to sell in fractions, or simply want the most recognizable, verifiable product.
- **Choose bars if** you're buying larger amounts, want the lowest cost per ounce, and are comfortable selling whole units to a reputable dealer.
- **Do both if** you want a base of bars for value plus some coins for flexibility — a common, sensible mix.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are gold coins or bars a better investment?**
Neither is inherently better. Bars cost less per ounce; coins are more liquid and easier to verify and divide. The "best" choice depends on how much you're buying and whether you value low cost or easy resale more.

**Why do gold coins cost more than bars?**
Coins cost more to mint, carry government backing and brand recognition, and are more liquid — so they command a higher premium over spot than plain bars.

**Are gold bars harder to sell than coins?**
Slightly. Bars from major refiners sell easily, but obscure bars may draw lower offers or require authentication. Common government coins are the easiest gold to sell anywhere.

**What size gold bar should I buy?**
For individuals, one-ounce and ten-ounce bars are the most liquid and practical. Very large bars cost the least per ounce but are hard to sell in part and harder to store.
