The gold price looks mysterious until you know what's pulling on it. **Gold moves mainly on six things: real interest rates, the strength of the U.S. dollar, inflation expectations, central-bank buying, fear, and the slow grind of supply and demand.** When several pull the same way, gold moves hard. When they fight each other, it drifts. This guide walks through each one in order of how much it usually matters, then shows how to read a gold quote so the headlines stop feeling random.

By the end you won't be able to predict next week — no one honestly can — but you'll understand *why* any given move is happening, which is the part that actually helps.

## 1. Real interest rates — the big one

The most important driver of gold is the real interest rate, and it's worth understanding first. **Gold pays no interest and no dividend, so its biggest rival is anything safe that does — like government bonds. When the real return on those safe assets falls, gold becomes more attractive; when it rises, gold suffers.**

The key word is *real* — the interest rate after inflation. A 5% bond yield with 2% inflation gives a real return of about 3%. The same 5% yield with 6% inflation gives a *negative* real return: you're losing purchasing power by playing it safe.

Here's why that matters for gold. Holding gold has an "opportunity cost" — the income you give up by parking money in metal instead of an interest-paying asset. **When real rates are high, that opportunity cost is painful and gold tends to struggle. When real rates are low or negative, the opportunity cost nearly vanishes, and gold's "it pays nothing" weakness stops being a weakness at all.** Most of gold's biggest rallies have coincided with falling or deeply negative real rates.

<Callout label="Key takeaway">
The single most useful gold question is: **"What are real interest rates doing?"** Falling real rates are a tailwind for gold. Rising real rates are a headwind. A surprising share of every big move traces back here.
</Callout>

## 2. The U.S. dollar

Gold and the dollar usually move in opposite directions, and the reason is mechanical. **Gold is priced in dollars worldwide, so when the dollar weakens, gold's dollar price tends to rise; when the dollar strengthens, gold tends to fall.**

There are two layers to it. First, the arithmetic: if the dollar loses value, it simply takes more dollars to buy the same ounce of metal, so the price ticks up. Second, demand: a cheaper dollar makes gold less expensive for buyers using euros, yen, or rupees, which lifts global demand and supports the price. This is why you'll see gold jump on a day the dollar slides even when there's no "gold news" at all. Traders watch the U.S. Dollar Index (DXY), a measure of the dollar against a basket of major currencies, as a quick read on this force.

## 3. Inflation and inflation expectations

Gold's reputation as an inflation hedge is real over long periods but unreliable over short ones. **Across decades, gold has broadly preserved purchasing power; across any given year, it can ignore inflation entirely or move on something else.**

What matters more than today's inflation figure is what markets *expect* inflation to do. Investors watch "breakeven" rates — the gap between regular Treasury yields and inflation-protected ones (TIPS) — as a live gauge of expected inflation. And notice how this loops back to driver #1: if people expect inflation to run hotter than central banks will offset with rate hikes, that points to lower *real* rates, which is the real engine. **Gold doesn't track the inflation headline so much as it tracks what inflation does to real returns.**

The slow part matters too. Over a single year, gold and inflation can easily diverge. Over twenty or thirty years, gold has tended to roughly keep pace with the cost of living — which is exactly why people hold it as ballast rather than as a short-term inflation trade.

## 4. Central-bank buying

The force most casual observers miss is the steady demand from central banks. **The world's central banks hold gold as a reserve asset, and in recent years many have been strong net buyers — adding more than 1,000 tonnes a year, according to the [World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org).** That's persistent, price-insensitive demand that doesn't care about daily headlines, and it puts a quiet floor under the market.

The motivation is part diversification and part politics: some nations want to hold less of their reserves in dollars and more in an asset no other government controls. When a major central bank announces large purchases, it's a genuine signal — not the noise that fear-based ads make it out to be, but a real, durable source of demand worth tracking.

## 5. Fear and uncertainty

Gold is the classic safe haven, and crises move it fast. **When a war breaks out, a banking scare spreads, or stock markets panic, money often rushes into gold for days or weeks, spiking the price.**

These moves are real, but they're usually temporary. Once the crisis cools, the fear premium tends to fade and the price settles back toward what the slower forces — rates, the dollar, real demand — justify. Fear spikes are the moves the doomsday ads love to point at, because they look dramatic in a chart. **Treat them as weather, not climate.** They tell you about this month's anxiety, not gold's underlying trend.

## 6. Supply and demand — the slow tide

Underneath the financial forces sits the physical market, and it moves slowly. **Gold supply grows only about 1%–2% a year, because mining is slow and almost all the gold ever mined still exists; demand, meanwhile, shifts between jewelry, investment, technology, and central banks.**

On the **supply** side, two sources feed the market: newly mined gold (the larger share) and recycled gold from old jewelry and electronics, which rises when prices are high. Neither responds quickly — opening a mine takes years — so supply can't surge to cap a rally the way it can with many commodities. That inelastic supply is part of why gold holds value.

On the **demand** side, the pie splits roughly into four pieces:

| Demand source | What it is | Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jewelry | Consumer demand, heavy in India and China | Falls when prices spike; seasonal |
| Investment | Bars, coins, and ETFs | Rises with fear and falling real rates |
| Central banks | Official reserves | Steady, large, price-insensitive |

The interplay matters: when investment and central-bank demand are both strong while supply barely grows, prices can run for a long time. When high prices choke off jewelry buying and pull recycled metal into the market, that acts as a brake.

## Putting it together

No single force is a crystal ball, and in the real world they overlap and sometimes cancel out. **The job isn't to predict next week — it's to understand which force is doing the pulling on any given move.**

<Figure caption="A simple read on the major forces. Real moves blend several at once.">

| Force | Gold tends to rise when… | Gold tends to fall when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real interest rates | Falling / negative | Rising / high |
| U.S. dollar | Weakening | Strengthening |
| Inflation expectations | Rising faster than rates | Cooling |
| Central-bank demand | Banks buying | Banks selling |
| Fear / crisis | Spiking | Calm returns |
| Supply & demand | Investment demand strong, supply flat | Demand soft, recycling high |

</Figure>

When you next read "gold hit a record," run down the list. Are real rates falling? Is the dollar sliding? Are central banks reported to be buying? Usually one or two of these explains the move, and the mystery dissolves.

## How to read a gold price quote

Gold is quoted in a few ways, and knowing them prevents confusion. **The headline number is almost always the spot price per troy ounce in U.S. dollars; from there you'll also see futures prices and per-gram or per-kilo quotes.**

- **Spot price** — the live wholesale price for immediate delivery, per troy ounce (about 31.1 grams). This is what most quotes mean.
- **Futures price** — the price for delivery on a set future date, traded on exchanges. It runs slightly above spot in normal conditions and is what financial news often shows.
- **The benchmark** — the LBMA Gold Price, set twice daily in London, is the global reference used for contracts and pricing.
- **Per gram / per kilo** — common outside the U.S.; just spot divided or multiplied accordingly.
- **Spot vs. what you pay** — remember the spot price is the wholesale number. As a buyer you pay spot *plus a premium*, covered in [how to buy gold](/guides/how-to-buy-gold).

## A short history of big gold moves

History is the best teacher here, because every major move maps back to the forces above. **Gold's largest rallies have lined up with falling real rates, a weak dollar, or genuine crisis — and its worst stretches with the opposite.** A quick tour:

- **The 1970s.** After the U.S. left the gold standard in 1971, gold went from \$35 an ounce to roughly \$800 by 1980. The driver was textbook: runaway inflation and deeply negative real rates, the most powerful setup gold can have.
- **The early 1980s slump.** When the Federal Reserve crushed inflation by pushing interest rates sky-high, real rates turned strongly positive — and gold fell for the better part of two decades.
- **2001–2011.** A long bull market carried gold from under \$300 to a then-record near \$1,900. Falling real rates, a weakening dollar, the 2008 financial crisis, and the launch of gold ETFs (which made buying easy) all pushed the same direction.
- **2011–2015.** As the crisis faded and markets expected higher rates, gold gave back a large share of those gains — the calm-returns side of the fear trade.
- **2020 onward.** Pandemic-era money printing, negative real rates, and broad uncertainty drove gold to fresh records, with steady central-bank buying adding a floor underneath.

Notice the pattern: **the same handful of forces explain decade-long trends and single-day moves alike.** The figures here are illustrative of direction, not precise marks — but the causes repeat.

## Gold versus other assets

Gold's value comes partly from how it behaves *differently* from everything else in a portfolio. **It tends to be lowly or negatively correlated with stocks over the long run, which is the whole point of holding it as ballast.** A quick comparison with the assets people weigh it against:

- **Gold vs. stocks.** Stocks pay dividends and grow with the economy; gold pays nothing and doesn't. But when stocks fall hard, gold often holds or rises, which is why a small gold position can steady a stock-heavy portfolio.
- **Gold vs. bonds.** Bonds are gold's direct rival for "safe money," because they pay interest. The contest is decided by real rates: when bonds offer a strong real yield, they win; when that yield is thin or negative, gold competes well.
- **Gold vs. the dollar.** Holding cash is safe in nominal terms but loses value to inflation over time. Gold has historically preserved purchasing power across decades, which is why some treat it as "money that can't be printed."
- **Gold vs. Bitcoin.** Both are pitched as alternatives to government money, and both can spike on the same fears. The difference is track record and volatility: gold has thousands of years of history and far milder swings, while Bitcoin is young and dramatically more volatile.

None of this makes gold "better" than these assets — it makes it *different*, and difference is exactly what diversification needs.

## Why gold reacts to Federal Reserve decisions

If you only watch one scheduled event for gold, watch the Federal Reserve. **Fed decisions on interest rates move gold because they act directly on the most important driver — real rates — and on the dollar at the same time.**

The reaction often comes in two parts. When the Fed *raises* rates or signals it will stay high, real returns on safe assets improve and the dollar tends to firm — both headwinds, so gold frequently dips. When the Fed *cuts* or signals easier policy ahead, the opposite: lower real yields and a softer dollar, both tailwinds, so gold often climbs. What's striking is that gold frequently moves on the *expectation* before the meeting and then again on the Fed chair's tone afterward, even if the rate itself lands where everyone predicted. That's markets pricing the future, not just the present. You'll see the same logic play out around major inflation reports, for the same reason — they shift what the Fed is expected to do next.

## Who actually sets the gold price?

No single person or country sets the gold price; it emerges from a few connected global markets trading around the clock. **The price you see comes mainly from the London over-the-counter market and the COMEX futures exchange, with the twice-daily LBMA benchmark serving as the official reference.** Knowing the pieces helps you read the news correctly.

- **The London OTC market** is the heart of physical gold trading — large, wholesale, bank-to-bank deals. The [LBMA](https://www.lbma.org.uk) runs the price "fixings" twice a day that become the global benchmark for contracts.
- **COMEX futures** (in the U.S.) is where much of the *price discovery* happens minute to minute, as traders buy and sell contracts for future delivery. The futures price and the spot price stay tightly linked through arbitrage.
- **Around-the-clock trading** means gold prices move overnight too, as markets in Asia, Europe, and the Americas hand off to each other. A move can happen while you sleep.

You'll sometimes hear about "paper gold versus physical gold" — the idea that far more gold trades on paper (futures and contracts) than exists in vaults. It's true that paper volumes dwarf physical metal, and it can amplify short-term moves. But over any meaningful period, the paper and physical prices track each other closely, because contracts can be settled in real metal. For a long-term holder, it's interesting background, not a reason to panic.

## Common myths about the gold price

A few stubborn myths cloud how people read gold, and clearing them up sharpens your judgment. **Gold is not a guaranteed inflation hedge in the short run, it does not only go up, and no single force controls it.**

- **Myth: "Gold always rises with inflation."** Over decades it broadly keeps pace; over a given year it often doesn't, because real rates — not the inflation headline — are the real driver.
- **Myth: "Gold only goes up."** Gold fell for most of the 1980s and 1990s and gave back large gains after 2011. It's a diversifier, not a one-way bet.
- **Myth: "The economy collapsing is good for gold."** Fear spikes help temporarily, but in the first days of a severe crash gold can fall too, as investors sell everything to raise cash.
- **Myth: "One bank or country controls the price."** Gold trades globally across multiple markets; no single actor sets it, though large central-bank buying genuinely influences the trend.

Spotting these myths in an ad is a quick way to tell honest analysis from a sales pitch.

## Why predictions fail (and what to do instead)

Honest people admit that short-term gold timing is mostly luck. **The forces above interact in ways no one reliably predicts, and the fear-driven spikes are the least predictable of all.** Forecasters who sound certain are selling certainty, not delivering it.

That's why most people who own gold treat it as a long-held diversifier rather than a trade — a position sized to a small slice of a portfolio and left alone, not something to flip on a hunch. Understanding the drivers helps you stay calm through the swings, which is worth more than any forecast.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the price of gold going up?**
Usually because one or more of its drivers turned favorable: real interest rates fell, the dollar weakened, central banks bought, or a crisis sent money searching for safety. Look at those first before reaching for a dramatic explanation.

**Does gold go up when the stock market crashes?**
Often, but not always. Gold frequently rises during panics as a safe haven, but in the first days of a severe crash it can dip too, as investors sell whatever they can to raise cash — before it typically recovers.

**Is gold a good hedge against inflation?**
Over long periods, yes — it has broadly preserved purchasing power. Over a single year it's unreliable, because what really drives it is *real* interest rates, not the inflation headline by itself.

**What is the difference between spot price and the price I pay?**
The spot price is the wholesale market price. As a retail buyer you pay spot plus a premium that covers minting, the dealer's margin, and shipping — typically a few percent on common bullion.

**Why do central banks buy gold?**
To diversify their reserves and hold an asset no other government controls. Many have reduced their reliance on the dollar, and gold is the natural alternative — which adds steady demand to the market.

**What time of year is gold usually cheapest?**
There are mild seasonal patterns — physical demand often picks up around Indian wedding and festival season and the Western holidays, which can firm prices late in the year. But the effect is small and unreliable next to rates, the dollar, and crises, so it's a footnote, not a strategy.

**Does a strong U.S. economy push gold down?**
Often, indirectly. A strong economy tends to bring higher interest rates and a firmer dollar, both headwinds for gold. A weakening economy tends to bring rate cuts and safe-haven buying, both tailwinds.

**Can the gold price be manipulated?**
Short-term moves can be nudged by large traders, and regulators have penalized "spoofing" in futures markets before. But over any meaningful period the price reflects the real forces in this guide — rates, the dollar, and genuine supply and demand — which no single actor can override for long.

**Why does gold sometimes fall when there's bad news?**
In the first hours of a severe sell-off, investors often dump everything — including gold — to raise cash and cover losses elsewhere. It's usually temporary; once the scramble for cash passes, gold's safe-haven role tends to reassert itself.
