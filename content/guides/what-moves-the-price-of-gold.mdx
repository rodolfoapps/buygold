The gold price looks mysterious until you know what's pulling on it. **Gold mostly moves on five forces: real interest rates, the strength of the U.S. dollar, inflation expectations, central-bank buying, and plain fear.** When several pull the same direction, gold moves hard. When they fight each other, it drifts. Here's each one in order of how much it usually matters.

## 1. Real interest rates — the big one

Gold pays you nothing. It earns no interest and no dividend. So its biggest rival is anything safe that *does* pay — like government bonds.

What matters is the **real** interest rate: the bond yield minus inflation. When real rates are high, holding cash-like bonds is attractive and gold tends to struggle. When real rates are low or negative — you're earning less than inflation by playing it safe — gold's "it pays nothing" problem disappears, and it tends to rise.

<Callout>
The single most useful gold question is: **"What are real interest rates doing?"** Falling real rates are a tailwind for gold. Rising real rates are a headwind. Most big moves trace back here.
</Callout>

## 2. The U.S. dollar

Gold is priced in dollars worldwide, so the two tend to move opposite each other. **When the dollar weakens, gold usually rises; when the dollar strengthens, gold usually falls.**

The logic is simple. If the dollar drops, it takes more dollars to buy the same ounce — so the dollar price goes up. It also makes gold cheaper for buyers using other currencies, which lifts demand. This is why you'll see gold jump on days the dollar slides, even with no "gold news" at all.

## 3. Inflation and inflation expectations

Gold's old reputation is "an inflation hedge," and there's truth in it — but it's slower and messier than the slogans suggest. Over decades, gold has broadly held purchasing power. Over any given year, it can ignore inflation entirely or move on something else.

What matters more than today's inflation number is what people *expect*. If markets believe inflation will run hot and central banks won't fully offset it, that points to lower real rates — and we're back to force #1.

## 4. Central-bank buying

This is the force most casual observers miss. The world's central banks hold gold as a reserve, and in recent years many have been steady net buyers — adding hundreds of tonnes a year. That's real, persistent demand that doesn't care about daily headlines, and it puts a quiet floor under the price. When a major central bank announces large purchases, it's a genuine signal worth noting.

## 5. Fear and uncertainty

Gold is the classic "safe haven." When a war breaks out, a banking scare spreads, or markets panic, money often rushes into gold for a few days or weeks. **These fear spikes are real but usually temporary** — they fade once the crisis cools. They're the moves the doomsday ads love to point at. Treat them as weather, not climate.

## Putting it together

<Figure caption="A simple read on the major forces. Real-world moves blend several at once.">

| Force | Gold tends to rise when… | Gold tends to fall when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real interest rates | Falling / negative | Rising / high |
| U.S. dollar | Weakening | Strengthening |
| Inflation expectations | Rising faster than rates | Cooling |
| Central-bank demand | Banks buying | Banks selling |
| Fear / crisis | Spiking | Calm returns |

</Figure>

No one of these is a crystal ball, and they overlap. The point isn't to predict next week — it's to understand *why* a move is happening so the headlines stop feeling random. When you read "gold hit a record," you'll now ask the useful question: which of the five forces is doing the pulling?

## A word on timing

Knowing what moves gold is not the same as timing it, and honest people admit that short-term timing is mostly luck. Most who own gold treat it as a long-held diversifier, not a trade.
